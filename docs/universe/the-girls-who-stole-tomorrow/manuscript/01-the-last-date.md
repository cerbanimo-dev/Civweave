# Chapter One — The Last Date on the Calendar
*The Girls Who Stole Tomorrow · The Blade & Mirror Saga*

The first thing I lost when tomorrow disappeared was a dentist appointment.

I didn't particularly mind losing the dentist appointment. I minded the way it happened.

My phone calendar had room for Thursday. Thursday was full of things I had been pretending I would get around to: a grocery run, the dentist, a reminder to call somebody about the dripping kitchen tap. At the bottom of the screen was a little gray line that read **END OF AVAILABLE DATES**.

I scrolled.

Thursday bounced back at me.

I turned the phone sideways, the way people do when a machine is being unreasonable and you hope changing your relationship to gravity will help.

Nothing.

“Could be an update,” Delia suggested.

The suggestion arrived in that familiar internal voice, close enough to be mine that I still sometimes caught myself answering aloud, different enough that silence between us had become its own kind of courtesy.

“Would an update remove Friday?”

“Depends how badly they wanted you to enjoy Thursday.”

I opened the calendar in a browser. It stopped at Thursday too. The year wasn't missing, exactly. The little arrows kept making their clicking motions. Every attempt to move ahead brought back the same week, with the same square of gray at the end.

My rune stone rested against my collarbone, warm from my skin. I rubbed a thumb over the shallow carved mark and went to the kitchen.

The paper calendar by the refrigerator had been there for months, mostly because it featured an improbable photograph of a cat who looked as though he owned a small bank. I tore off Thursday's page.

Underneath it was cardboard.

There should have been Friday. There should have been weeks of little cats exercising their modest financial ambitions.

Instead, there was the advertisement for the company that printed the thing, followed by a white space too clean to be accidental.

I checked the wastebasket. The previous pages were there. The back of Wednesday promised Thursday. Thursday promised nothing.

“Now,” Delia said, “that's interesting.”

I wanted to say something clever. I made coffee instead.

***

By nine, I had three witnesses and one extremely unhelpful explanation.

The first witness was the woman at the corner bakery, who held up a handwritten sign announcing that birthday cakes could be ordered for any date up to midnight. She didn't understand why the booking form had printed it that way. She was furious with the printer. The second was a customer whose train ticket showed a departure time but no arrival date. The third was a man outside the pharmacy arguing with his watch.

The explanation came from a television above the bakery counter.

A smiling presenter assured us that several major scheduling services were experiencing “temporary future-display irregularities.”

He managed to say this without laughing.

On the television, a banner beneath his face listed emergency numbers, an instruction not to panic, and an appointment for a press conference at a time that was already in the past.

I bought a roll I didn't want.

“You're gathering evidence,” Delia said.

“I'm gathering carbohydrates.”

“That too.”

My phone rang.

Maggie.

I answered before the second vibration.

“Please tell me your calendar works.”

“If tomorrow has been discontinued,” Maggie said, “I'd like to speak to the manager.”

“So that's a no.”

“Magnus is running tests. I am providing emotional support by threatening the equipment.”

A muffled voice in the background said, “You're holding my phone upside down.”

“It's more expressive this way.”

I smiled despite myself. “Does he have anything?”

Maggie took a moment before answering. “He wants to talk. You good with that?”

“Of course.”

The line rustled. A breath. Then Magnus spoke, his voice quieter, precise in that way he got when frightened and trying to be useful.

“Landry. Tell me you've checked a paper calendar.”

“I ripped a cat in half.”

“What?”

“Calendar. Cat calendar.”

“Oh, good. That would have been a difficult follow-up question.”

He described what he'd tried. Two phones without network access. An old appointment diary. A train timetable printed last winter. The devices could show their current time, and the older diary still contained pages in the future, but every new request to schedule something beyond midnight failed.

“So it isn't every physical page,” I said.

“No. Which matters. We don't get to say time is ending because a few machines have developed an opinion about Friday.”

“The bakery calendar is blank.”

“Newly printed?”

“Last month.”

He went quiet.

“We need a third kind of evidence,” he said. “Something that didn't come from a calendar company, a printer, or a phone. Something whose history we can check.”

A car horn sounded outside. Another car answered. The world went on doing ordinary things, almost offensively.

“What happens at midnight?”

“I don't know,” Magnus said.

The honesty helped.

***

The child was sitting at a booth in a diner that had no business being open on the wrong side of a strange morning.

I went there because I needed a chair and the comforting sight of somebody frying potatoes. The little bell over the door gave a tired ring as I entered. At the counter, a waitress was trying to explain to a delivery driver why the register would not print orders for tomorrow. A line of customers had developed that particular expression people get when they're deciding whether a disaster entitles them to free coffee.

The girl occupied the last booth.

Seven, perhaps. Small enough that her feet swung above the floor. Brown hair clipped away from her eyes. Blueberry syrup on one cuff. She had arranged six sugar packets in a perfect line beside her pancakes.

There was a seventh packet on the seat opposite.

She looked up.

“You're late,” she said.

I stopped. “Do I know you?”

“Yes.”

I waited.

She sliced a pancake into four equal pieces, then looked annoyed with herself and cut one of the pieces in half.

“Did you come by yourself?” I asked.

“Did you?”

“I'm not sure that answers my question.”

“It answers a better one.”

Delia made a small sound of appreciation.

The girl's gaze flicked toward my shoulder. I hadn't moved.

“Is she here?” the girl asked.

I took a breath.

“Who?”

“The person you almost asked permission to talk for.”

That was enough to make me sit down. It was not enough to make me trust her.

“I don't know how you know that,” I said.

“I keep telling you.”

“Then give me a chance to hear it.”

Her expression changed. Some of the practiced composure slipped, and for a moment she looked like a child who had stayed up too late and wanted somebody sensible to arrive.

“I've already saved you six times,” she said. “You're making number seven very difficult.”

“Six times from what?”

“Different things.”

“Such as?”

She touched the row of sugar packets. Her finger hovered over the first and moved to the sixth without stopping.

“Every time I tell you, you start trying to fix the parts that haven't happened.”

“That sounds like something I might do.”

“I know.”

I put my hands flat on the table.

The girl glanced at them, then at my necklace. She didn't reach for either.

“Can I ask your name?”

“You can.”

“Will you tell me?”

“Maybe after you listen.”

The waitress appeared, set down a mug of coffee I hadn't ordered, and gave the girl a look of mild concern.

“Everything okay, honey?”

The girl nodded. “I'm getting better at this.”

“That makes one of us,” the waitress said, and went to confront the register again.

I could have told the waitress something was wrong. I could have asked her to stay. I had no evidence the child was in danger, beyond the fact that every grown-up in town had been suddenly deprived of the ability to make plans. I kept my hands on the table and asked a smaller question.

“Did you leave someone waiting for you?”

“No.”

“Does someone know where you are?”

“Yes.”

“Would you like me to call them?”

“No, thank you.”

Three answers. One refusal. I could work with those.

“Okay,” I said.

The child stared as if she had expected a fight.

Delia spoke softly. “Ask about the photograph.”

“What photograph?” I asked her silently.

Then I saw it.

The girl's syrup-sticky fingers had kept one corner of a rectangular piece of paper pinned beneath the plate. She slid it toward me, slowly enough that I could have refused to look.

It was a photograph.

Four people stood in front of a building made of dark glass and pale stone. There were trees on either side, their leaves caught halfway between green and gold. I recognized Magnus at once, looking up as though somebody had called to him from an upper window. Maggie stood beside him, grinning with the sideways mischief I'd heard on the phone.

I was there too.

My hand was raised. The familiar rune stone hung at my throat.

And Delia stood next to me.

Separate. Solid. Her own face. Her own shadow.

For a long second I couldn't make my fingers work.

“Landry?” Delia asked.

“I see you.”

“I can tell.”

“I mean there. In the picture.”

The girl watched without interrupting.

I had spent years learning which things I could share with Delia and which things belonged entirely to one of us. A photograph of us standing side by side should have been the beginning of a thousand questions. It was the first thing I didn't know how to ask.

The building wasn't familiar. Or perhaps it was the kind of unfamiliar that makes you certain you will recognize it one day.

There should have been a fifth person in the group.

I knew because the spacing was wrong. My raised hand pointed toward an empty patch of sky. Maggie's arm stopped at the edge of a neat, deliberate opening in the photograph.

Somebody had cut a face out.

Not torn the picture in anger. Cut it carefully. There was a faint curve of scissors through the paper, almost tender in its precision.

A fruit fly landed on the far edge of the photograph.

The child lifted one finger to shoo it away, then reconsidered. The fly stepped along the cut edge and vanished into the white space beneath the picture.

“Don't squish it,” the girl said.

“I wasn't going to.”

“Good. They're useful.”

The fly reappeared near my coffee, paused as if evaluating the service, and took off.

I turned the photograph over.

There was no date on the back. Only five words written in blue ink:

**ONE FUTURE. GUARANTEED. NO RETURNS.**

Underneath, in much smaller writing, was a number.

Seven.

The girl pulled the photograph back before I could copy it.

“Now do you see?” she asked.

“No,” I said. “I see evidence that somebody has been here before us. I see four people I know, and somebody they made an effort to remove. I see a little girl who knows things she shouldn't. I'm going to need more.”

She studied me.

“That's a different answer.”

“From what?”

“The other six times.”

I looked at the seventh sugar packet. At the old photograph. At the perfectly ordinary sunlight coming through the diner window.

It occurred to me that I had spent the whole morning asking what would happen to *my* tomorrow.

The girl had been asking who would be allowed to have one.

“Do you want to tell me who made the photograph?” I asked.

Her shoulders lowered a fraction.

“Yes,” she said. “But first I need you to promise something.”

“I don't promise things before I hear them.”

For the first time, she smiled.

“Good,” she said. “That's why I came back.”

The television above the counter clicked off. Every screen in the diner went dark, then lit again with the same black words on white:

**BIDDING OPENS AT MIDNIGHT.**

There were still hours left in the day.

I had never been more relieved to have time to think.
