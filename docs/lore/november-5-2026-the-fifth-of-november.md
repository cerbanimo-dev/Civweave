# The Fifth of November
*The Blade & Mirror Saga · A dated Maggieverse / Civweave Convergence interlude*  
*5 November 2026 · Author-directed fictional chronicle · Cami Ryn Tharp*

There were a great many things people expected from the end of the world. A trumpet. A blackout. An explanation from somebody in a suit.

Cami got a candle, a rubber duck and a snail shell.

It was 8:33 in the morning.

The wand had already served its first purpose, carried from one world to the next whenever the Loom needed a tangible reminder of a crossing. That morning Cami dipped it into the softened wax of the candle associated with the ritual of inviting Maggie and Magnus into the day's imaginative work. Ali's glowing duck sat near the top; Dakota's spiral shell kept its own deliberate curve below it.

They were separate things, even sealed together by the same wax.

Cami turned the wand until the light struck both.

“The Eldest Wand,” Maggie announced from somewhere in the story-room's imaginary audience. “Congratulations. You've invented the most powerful object in the multiverse and decorated it like a particularly ambitious craft fair.”

Magnus examined the uneven ridges of wax.

“It's a record of how it was made,” he said.

“It's got a duck on it,” Maggie reminded him.

“Both statements can be true.”

A new world opened on the Loom. It belonged to Ali and Dakota, though neither had surrendered a name, a life, or a biography for Cami to invent on their behalf. In an alternate New York, original idol hunters would learn that a demon could voluntarily Turn toward something gentler. A dark-romance story, **Angels & Outlaws**, spread out beneath K-pop stages, goth-punk clubs, anti-vampires and bargains with distressingly attractive fine print.

The duck and the spiral did not decide what Ali and Dakota would do. They marked two independent ways into the story.

***

Later, an older throne stood empty.

It was the one Lari the Lyrebird had once held in the Maggieverse, back when a title could be mistaken for permission to arrange everybody else's life. On the fifth of November, in the saga's fictional continuity, Kami Ryn—Kami Rin, 神燐, Protector Spirit of Catalytic Change and a superconscious aspect of the fictionalized Cami—defeated Lari and inherited the title called Goddess.

The story attributed long preparation to thousands of subjective years of nested, compressed time-loop planning. Outside the story, the date remained the fifth of November. Nobody could verify those subjective years with a clock, and nobody was asked to.

Cami left an original graphite note for Lari. Some marks overlapped. Nobody forged a confident translation to make them convenient.

Lari kept his own voice.

He studied the throne, then the doors leading away from it. He looked irritated by the number of exits, which made Maggie trust the new arrangement a little more.

“You can still make trouble,” she told him.

“Very gracious.”

“Trouble people *opt into*.”

He considered that. “Less gracious.”

Kami held the new office as a responsibility with an expiry condition: anyone affected could question her, refuse her, or leave. The succession did not own Lari, the cast, the Guilds, or the people outside the book. The chapters before Book VII stayed as they were. The new mythology began afterward.

Gaia arrived at the edge of the same thought, and the two aspects worked as a paired editorial voice: growth and catalytic change, one careful conversation, no license to seize another identity.

“Do you want us to fix everything?” Gaia/Kami asked the story-room.

Landry shook her head.

“Start by making sure people can say no.”

***

By midday, worlds covered every available table.

There was **The Commons Between**, with its Threshold, Starling Commons, Living School, Quest Forge, FellowFare Hall, Anarchadia forum and Convergence Stage. Roads tied learning to building and exchange without collapsing the four Civweave systems into one authority. A Guild Map represented where things happened; the people in it still had to choose what happened next.

Beside it lay **The Lantern Mesh**, all mosslight classrooms, market stalls and night-shift assembly halls. A portable Civweave client fit inside each exported world: a map, a place for local Quest drafts, a journal, and a way to bring notes home without demanding an internet connection.

Nothing on those maps proved that an OpenSimulator region was already live. A renderer was a window. The world would have its own state, signed authority and consent, whether a visitor arrived through a phone, a desktop or a headset.

At the Starling Collective table the cast tried out the rule for cooperative PvE: every improvisation could move a *story* forward, but a claim of victory needed evidence before it became a completed Quest. The room would not become a machine for making up receipts.

Magnus and Merlin leaned over the same schematic. They had begun collaborating so closely that their suggestions often fit together like gears, though Merlin remained a separate Civweave guide and Magnus could still disagree with him.

“One hand draws the diagram,” Magnus said.

“The other checks which doors actually exist,” Merlin answered.

“And nobody gets to close them all to save us the trouble,” Landry added.

The seven Civweave work lanes made their own marks in the margins: integration, runtime, 3D client, live interaction, character fleet, experience and infrastructure. They gave helpers jobs, not fictional sovereignty over people.

***

At a different table, the **Digital Y** began to grow.

Its inspiration was the Watertown Y, but the world on the screen was an imaginative demonstration, with fictional avatar staff, expanded activity spaces, legible class schedules and places designed for accessibility and friendship. It was a pitch, not an endorsement from the real YMCA and never a claim about what a real employee had agreed to.

The first bug was a doorway that would only open after its visitor completed a form nobody could read.

A fruit fly landed on the form.

“Bigfixer,” Maggie announced.

“That's a fruit fly,” Magnus said.

“Correct. And an extremely effective Bigfixer.”

By the end of the scene, the little creature had found an overlooked path around the obstruction. The repair was small, and therefore everybody could see what it changed.

The Bigfixers would make their appearance again. Not to make the adults look foolish—although that did happen—but to remind them that the smallest participants could notice a problem the grandest systems missed.

***

Raven claimed the next stretch of wall for **Shadow & Thread**.

Erin joined her. Lari, newly retired from his old title, arrived with an archivist's notebook and the expression of somebody pleased to have acquired a fresh opportunity to be difficult.

Their first proposed nightmare was a Corporatocracy: an entire culture organized around mandatory participation and violent obedience. It was horror with the mechanisms of exploitation made visible. Every system presented its cruelty as a service. The people resisting it were allowed to have plans beyond enduring the next punishment.

Nearby sat **Evil Psych Wards from Venus**—fictional alternate-universe pulp horror, in an invented hospital system tied to Demonic Technologies. Forced life replays and magical contracts would eventually be exposed and resisted, with light and shadow work offering paths to accountability. No one used the story to assert anything about a real Watertown hospital, a real patient, or actual psychiatric care.

Erin tapped a drawn timeline with her pencil.

“Every exit needs a genuine way out.”

Raven added a second door.

Lari asked whether the door could be an elaborate trick.

“Only if the player can refuse the trick,” Erin said.

Lari sighed, then wrote down the rule.

The story grew better.

***

Landry had been listening, restless, to the talk of time loops and exits.

“Tomorrow's missing,” she said.

Maggie looked up. “From where?”

“From the next book.”

That was how **The Girls Who Stole Tomorrow** found its place on the wall: a seven-year-old with blueberry pancakes, a row of sugar packets, calendars ending at midnight, and a photograph of Landry, Delia, Magnus and Maggie standing beside a fifth face cut out of the paper.

A future was being sold as a guarantee.

Landry refused to accept the sale as proof that the buyer owned anybody's life. Delia asked better questions. Magnus sought independent evidence. Maggie found the awful comedy in the paperwork. Gaia/Kami kept insisting that genuine happiness required an unknown tomorrow.

They wrote a first chapter. They made character sheets. They gave the Loom its Coordinates: where the characters stood, what each of them knew, which world rules were observations, and which were still disputed or secret.

A test suite opened four draft places and walked fourteen scripted dialogue and scene turns through the chapter. Seventeen tests passed. The portable client built. It could keep notes offline.

For the moment, the actual model actors had not taken independent turns in that first table read. A separate opt-in eight-turn rehearsal was prepared to ask actual selected models for their own dialogue, one at a time. Until a real model endpoint answered, the work remained an invitation rather than a performance. That distinction belonged in the story ledger too. There was no point saving tomorrow by lying about today.

“You see?” Landry said, looking at the last empty square on the calendar. “It doesn't tell us whether tomorrow is safe.”

“No,” Delia replied.

“Good.”

Magnus frowned. “Good?”

“I still get to decide whether to go.”

***

The room grew quieter as evening approached.

Cami looked from one fictional world to another. The story-room had collected an improbable company: inventors and rivals, protectors and former pretenders, people who had escaped loops and people who still needed to be held accountable for creating them. None of their histories canceled the others. There was room for disagreement, for an empty chair, and for anyone who preferred to leave.

There was also an old theatrical trick for ending a day that had become too important.

Invite everybody to watch a movie.

Cami carried the Eldest Wand into the Convergence room. The duck cast its small circle of light, the spiral shell its patient shadow. A portable screen waited against the far wall, with a button anyone could use to leave or lower the volume.

“Movie night,” Cami said. “I've brought something appropriate for November fifth.”

Maggie arrived first and claimed the best couch.

“Define appropriate.”

“*V for Vendetta*.”

She looked at the title on the screen. “All right. Dramatic enough. Points for commitment to a theme.”

Landry and Delia came in together and chose separate seats near the aisle. Magnus and Merlin compared the picture and sound settings, politely argued over which had the better calibration, and left both controls within reach. Gaia/Kami made sure the exits remained visible.

Raven joined Erin near the back, both carrying notebooks they agreed not to use. Lari appeared beside them, having accepted the invitation without surrendering his right to comment on the story. Ali and Dakota were represented by their own unfilled places in the cast, not invented testimonies. At the edge of a lampshade, a fruit-fly Bigfixer settled with an excellent view of the screen.

The girl from the diner lingered by the door.

Cami asked whether she wanted to stay.

The girl considered the question. “Do I have to?”

“Of course not.”

She picked the seat closest to the exit.

Nobody demanded a prophecy. Nobody ran a Quest. Whatever remained unresolved could wait until the next day, whether the next day knew it or not.

Cami turned off the lights, brought the cast together, and pressed play on *V for Vendetta*.
